The stateFlat XGBoost, and a ceiling made of missing measurements

Model B maps physiology and labs to per-hour sepsis risk. It is complete end to end on MIMIC‑IV 3.1, 63,672 episodes.

The best deployable configuration is flat XGBoost on 17 features with a pre-onset target and a utility-weighted training objective. Nine months of architecture work has produced no durable gain. Every gain came from changing the problem definition, the evaluation, or what the model is asked to care about.

The binding constraint is measurement sparsity, not model capacity: in the pre-onset window there are effectively three live signals — heart rate, SpO2 and respiratory rate at ~95% of hours — while every lab sits at 2–8%. That is the quantitative case for Model A, and as of this record it is finally being tested causally rather than asserted.

The pattern worth knowing first

Every model-side change tested before 2026‑09‑22 was null: architecture (0.010 AUROC across six configs), MAE pretraining, missingness encoding (0.007 across four arms), pos_weight, TLS on XGBoost, pooled multi-source pretraining. The single exception arrived last: weighting training hours by the warning they actually deliver, +0.042 capture ≥6h. Nothing about the model changed — only which hours it was asked to care about.

The cost, stated plainly

Patient precision falls 0.214 → 0.172, timestep precision 0.094 → 0.077, AUROC 0.720 → 0.663. At 0.172, roughly six of every seven alarmed patients are not septic. Under the settled product decision that is the trade we want, but it is a trade — and it makes precision, not capture, the axis that now needs work.

The eight surviving channels are heart rate, SpO2, respiratory rate, temperature, MAP, SBP, FiO2 and urine output — the continuously charted signals. That is the class a wearable produces.

TLS's ramp is backwards, and this was predicted before the run rather than read off afterwards. TLS peaks at onset and decays backwards, weighting the hours worth least for early warning. The prediction was that utility would beat TLS on capture while TLS looked relatively better on plain recall — which credits any lead above zero. Measured: TLS is −0.029 on capture but +0.012 on recall, and utility beats it by +0.055 [+0.030, +0.080] on capture and +2.96 h of lead. The prediction had room to fail.

AUROC moved the other way again — 0.7202 → 0.7140 while every deployment metric rose. That is the sixth independent instance.

Result — experiment 30bThe density ablation inverted the premise

First causal test of “the ceiling is measurement sparsity.” Stratifying patients by observed density cannot answer it — sicker patients get more labs, so density and severity are inseparable. This deletes observations at random from the same patients, so severity is held fixed by construction.

Deleting every lab improves early warning, monotonically. The project had spent months treating lab sparsity as the ceiling; causally, the labs were costing capture. The vitals are load-bearing — steeply and monotonically the other way.

AUROC falls in both directions, so the labs do carry discriminative signal. It arrives too late to be worth anything: removing them costs 5 points of AUROC and buys 4 of capture. Seventh instance of the anti-correlation.

Mechanism — hypothesis, not finding

A lab draw is a clinician action, and t_sepsis is partly a treatment-decision timestamp (culture drawn, antibiotics started). Lab presence therefore correlates with someone already being worried, which peaks near onset — so the model may be learning “labs are being drawn, therefore risk”, which maximises per-hour ranking and fires late.

If it holds it sharpens the wearable argument rather than weakening it. The problem is not that labs are sparse; it is that labs are sparse and their timing is confounded with clinical suspicion. Passive continuous sensing breaks that confound, because it measures whether or not anyone is worried. The clean test: thin the lab values while keeping the observation indicators, then the reverse.

Scope. These are the nine routine clinical labs. The result says nothing about IL‑6, procalcitonin or PTX3 — MIMIC‑IV contains none of them, so the biomarker hypothesis is untested here, not refuted.

MethodTwo burden confounds, one afternoon, two different wrong answers

Alert burden depends only on non-septic alarm-hours, so the threshold that hits a target burden is a quantile, not a search. Before that was implemented, the same comparison gave three answers:

  • Validation-frozen thresholds said pretraining hurts badly, −0.087 — but the pretrained arms had drifted 0.099–0.182 alerts/patient-day below scratch, and an arm that alarms less catches less.
  • Re-pinning on the 0.01 threshold grid flipped the sign to +0.021, because that grid is far too coarse at 2.6% prevalence.
  • Solving exactly gives the real answer: −0.020.

The same failure had already manufactured a CI-backed +0.028 TLS “gain” that was really +0.001. It is the ninth instance of this project's recurring bug class — a quantity optimised or compared on the same axis it is then reported on — and the fix is now mechanical rather than a matter of care.

A false positive caught by its control

The hospital-shortcut probe reads AUROC 0.989 on pretrained encoders, which looks damning. The random-encoder control reads 0.993. The two databases are separable from the raw inputs alone; pretraining moves it −0.004. Reporting the probe without the control would have invented a shortcut that does not exist.

Representation delivered the single largest win in the project — and it was almost entirely the forward-fill, which is already in every model. Every further attempt to represent irregularity better has returned nothing.

The honest split: reformatting and harmonising the data is a closed question. Deriving new physiological features from it is open and untested. Shock index, BUN/creatinine ratio, partial SOFA, SOFA deterioration deltas — relationships between channels that a tree must otherwise rediscover. The CinC 2019 winner got 0.389 → 0.418 from exactly this, and it is the one item in the techniques review never attempted here.

The honest partWhat we are struggling with

Seven open problems, ordered by how much they block the project rather than by how hard they are.

  1. Precision is now the binding problem, not capture. At 0.172 patient precision, roughly six of every seven alarmed patients are not septic. Every capture gain this month cost precision. Nothing tried so far moves precision without giving back capture — the two have traded in every experiment.
  2. The signal is weak everywhere, not just early. AUROC is 0.724 even in the final six hours before onset, where deterioration tasks with real physiological signal reach 0.85–0.90. No amount of modelling makes a weak signal strong, and this is the number that caps everything else.
  3. Part of the label is clinician behaviour. t_sepsis depends on when cultures were drawn and antibiotics started, so we are partly predicting when someone decided to act. The density ablation suggests the lab channels exploit exactly that. We do not have a label free of this, and MIMIC cannot provide one.
  4. There is no clean external validation. PhysioNet shares a hospital (BIDMC) with MIMIC, so it is not independent. SICdb has no microbiology, so its labels are a different definition and measured to over-call by 3–7×. Generalisation to a new site is therefore untested — not failed, untested.
  5. Model A has no paired data anywhere. Model A maps electrochemical sensor signal to biomarker concentration. No dataset we have found pairs the two. SICdb and Humanitas supply the concentration side only, so they can specify what a sensor must resolve but cannot train or calibrate one. This is the hard blocker on the actual thesis.
  6. Architecture is exhausted. Every model-side change was null until reweighting: six-config architecture sweep spanning 0.010 AUROC, MAE pretraining twice, missingness encoders, pooled multi-source pretraining, pos_weight, TLS on trees. The remaining headroom is not in the model.
  7. The measurement apparatus keeps being the bug — nine times. The recurring shape is a quantity optimised or compared on the same axis it is then reported on. It has produced a CI-backed gain that did not exist, a sign flip inside one afternoon, a 97%-scrambled patient attribution, and a 63% cohort loss. The rate is not obviously falling.
The working rule this produced

Assume the next surprising result is an evaluation artifact until a control says otherwise. Every check that has earned its place came from this: exact-burden thresholds, paired patient bootstraps, mass-matched arms, and random-encoder controls. Two of those caught false positives this week alone.

NextWhat could still move the number

  1. Conformal abstention. Now the largest untested lever: −78 to −86% false alarms, acting on the axis the product actually constrains. A rejected prediction is a delayed alarm, not a free one — rejected septic patients must stay in the denominator with their delay counted.
  2. Clinical derived features. Not reformatting. Encoding physiology between channels. Never attempted here.
  3. Feature ablation on the extended set. The 18 features went in as a block and nobody has checked which earn their place. MAP and urine output are the dense ones and the likely winners.
  4. Multi-source pretraining — tested, null (experiment 29).
  5. More measurement, i.e. Model A. The density ablation now running is the first causal test of whether that premise holds: same patients, observations deleted at random, so severity cannot confound it the way stratifying by observed density would.

Still openDecisions and traps

  • Episode-rate or patients-disturbed as the alert budget? Only the second actually constrains.
  • The Humanitas immortal-time trap. Zero of the 79 patients with a day‑5 sample died by day 5; 28‑day mortality is 9.4% against 43.1% without. Any trajectory-versus-outcome analysis needs a day‑5 landmark, which leaves 6 deaths at 28 days and 12 at 90 — one predictor, not nine.
  • SICdb sealed hold-out. 5,948 episodes never pretrained on, so the database survives as external validation. Once a patient is pretrained on, it cannot serve that role.
